# Slide 1 — The problem

**Slide title:** Nobody knows who to call this morning

**On the slide:** three lines, big.

> A rep has 500 accounts and no idea which three matter today.
> The signal is public — in filings, newsrooms, job ads.
> Reading it by hand does not scale, and guessing costs the meeting.

---

## What to say

*~60 seconds. Slow down here; everything else is the answer to this.*

"Every B2B seller has the same morning problem. You have a book of a few
hundred accounts, and you have maybe twenty calls in you this week. The question
is not *how do I sell* — it is *who do I call first*, and it is asked every
single morning with no good answer.

The answer is already public. When a company announces a cost programme with a
number attached, when it starts hiring for a capability it does not have, when
it puts a compliance deadline in a filing — that is published, in the open, days
or weeks before anyone picks up the phone. It is the earliest signal there is.

But nobody reads five hundred companies' worth of it. So the choice gets made on
relationships and recency and whoever emailed last, and the account that
actually announced something on Tuesday gets called in three weeks, after the
budget has been assigned.

And here is the part that matters for what we built: **the failure is not a lack
of information. It is that the information is unstructured.** The signal is one
sentence in a 7,700-character results announcement. A person can find it. A
spreadsheet cannot."

## The trap to name out loud

If there is time, name the obvious wrong answer, because a judge will think of it:

"The obvious answer is to point a language model at the internet and ask it who
to call. We think that is the wrong answer, and the rest of this is why. A model
that gives you a name and a reason you cannot check is not a sales tool. It is a
very confident intern."

## Transition

"That is the problem. Here is what we do about it — and the first thing we do is
refuse to let the model make anything up."
